The science of playful learning

Kids learn best when they’re playing, not memorizing.

Children are born scientists, builders and storytellers. Decades of research show that hands-on, joyful, social play builds deeper understanding than drilling facts from a textbook or sitting through a lecture. Don’t just read about it — play your way through the evidence.

Why play?

Sit still. Listen. Memorize. Forget.

For generations, learning has often meant copying from the board, re-reading the textbook and listening quietly. It looks orderly — but knowledge that’s only been heard or crammed fades fast. Children remember what they do, discover and talk about.

Why it works

On a straight, numbered board, bigger numbers are further along and take more hops and more counting to reach. Preschoolers who played this game for about an hour got better at counting, reading, comparing and estimating numbers — and still had the gains 9 weeks later. The same game with colors instead of numbers didn’t help.

Try it at home: draw 10 squares in a row, flip a coin (heads = 1, tails = 2) and race. Keep it straight — a circular board didn’t work as well.

What makes play work

The 5 ingredients of playful learning

Researchers reviewing the evidence found that children learn most when an experience is…

  1. 😄

    Joyful

    Delight and surprise grab attention and keep children coming back for more. Fun isn’t the opposite of learning — it’s the fuel.

  2. 🧠

    Actively engaging

    Minds-on, not just hands-on: children think, choose and do instead of passively receiving.

  3. 🔗

    Meaningful

    New ideas stick when they connect to things children already know and care about.

  4. 🔁

    Iterative

    Try, wobble, tweak, try again. Play makes mistakes safe — and mistakes are where learning happens.

  5. 🤝

    Socially interactive

    Explaining, debating and sharing with others deepens understanding — and builds social skills too.

Source: Zosh et al. (2017), Learning through play: a review of the evidence, The LEGO Foundation.

    The evidence

    What the research actually says

    Real studies in plain language, with links so you can check our homework.

    University · 225 studies

    1.5×

    Lectures make failing more likely

    Across 225 studies of university science and maths courses, students in traditional lecture classes were 1.5 times more likely to fail than students in classes with active learning. Exam scores rose by about 6% with active learning.

    Freeman et al. (2014), PNAS · Read the study ↗

    Ages 4–5 · Randomized experiment

    ~1 hour

    A board game builds number sense

    Preschoolers from low-income families played a simple 1–10 number board game in four 15–20-minute sessions. They got better at counting, reading numbers, comparing and estimating — and the gains lasted 9 weeks. Classmates who played the same game with colors instead of numbers didn’t improve.

    Ramani & Siegler (2008), Child Development · Read the study ↗

    Ages 1–8 · Systematic review

    39 studies

    Guided play beats direct instruction for early maths

    A 2022 review of 39 studies (17 pooled in a meta-analysis) found guided play had a greater positive effect than direct instruction on early maths skills, shape knowledge and task switching — a key thinking skill.

    Skene et al. (2022), Child Development · Read the study ↗

    Students · Memory experiment

    61% vs 40%

    Recalling beats re-reading

    A week after studying a text, students who had practised recalling it remembered 61% of its ideas; students who simply re-read it remembered 40%. Quizzes, games and challenges are packed with this kind of retrieval practice.

    Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science · Read the study ↗

    Meta-analysis · 77 comparisons

    5,547 learners

    Learning games boost learning and memory

    Serious games led to more learning and better retention than conventional instruction — especially when combined with other teaching, played over several sessions and played in groups.

    Wouters et al. (2013), Journal of Educational Psychology · Read the study ↗

    Preschoolers · Experiment

    Told ≠ taught

    Showing the answer can shrink curiosity

    When an adult demonstrated one function of a new toy, preschoolers explored more narrowly and discovered fewer of its other hidden functions than children who weren’t taught. Instruction is efficient — but it can switch off exploration.

    Bonawitz et al. (2011), Cognition · Read the study ↗

    Ages 4–5 · Experiment

    3 ways tested

    Shapes stick better through guided play

    Seventy 4- and 5-year-olds learned about shapes through guided play, free play or direct instruction. The guided-play group understood shapes best — and the advantage was still there a week later.

    Fisher et al. (2013), Child Development · Read the study ↗

    Pediatrics · Clinical report

    ℞ Play

    Doctors are told to “prescribe” play

    The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages a “prescription for play” at well-child visits, stating: “Play is not frivolous: it enhances brain structure and function and promotes executive function.”

    Yogman et al. (2018), Pediatrics · Read the report ↗

    Honesty corner

    The fine print

    • The strongest evidence for school skills is for guided play — a playful activity with a learning goal and a supportive adult — not for leaving kids entirely alone with toys.
    • Effects vary: the 2022 review found no clear difference between guided play and direct instruction for literacy or social-emotional outcomes.
    • Many lecture-vs-active studies involve university students, and some play studies are small.
    • Play works best alongside good teaching, not instead of it.

    Bring it home

    Playful ideas for every age

    No special kit required — just a little imagination. Pick an age group:

    🐸10 min

    Number line hop

    Chalk or tape ten squares numbered 1–10. Flip a coin — heads hop 1, tails hop 2 — and shout each number as you land.

    Learns: counting, number order, “bigger = further”

    🔺15 min

    Shape detectives

    Cut out real triangles and sneaky impostors (curvy sides, open corners). Can they find the real ones and explain the secret rule?

    Learns: shapes, reasoning, describing

    🛁15 min

    Sink or float lab

    Fill a tub with water and gather ten household objects. Predict, test, then sort them into “sinkers” and “floaters”. Any surprises?

    Learns: predicting, testing, early science

    🪨10 min

    Story stones

    Draw simple pictures on pebbles. Pull out three and invent a story together — then swap who starts.

    Learns: vocabulary, sequencing, imagination

    🍕20 min

    Pizza fractions

    Cut paper-plate pizzas into halves, thirds and quarters. Share them fairly between friends, then debate: is ⅓ or ¼ bigger?

    Learns: fractions, fairness, comparing

    🃏15 min

    Times-table war

    Each player flips two cards and multiplies. The biggest product wins the round. Start with just the 2s, 5s and 10s.

    Learns: multiplication fluency, patterns

    🔤20 min

    Spelling treasure hunt

    Hide letter tiles around the room. Teams race to find them and build this week’s words, then write the silliest sentence they can.

    Learns: spelling, teamwork, sentence building

    🌱2 weeks

    Bean detectives

    Plant beans in clear jars: one in the dark, one without water, one on the windowsill. Measure each day and chart the race.

    Learns: fair tests, measuring, graphs

    🛒45 min

    Classroom market

    Kids price products, get a budget and run a market. Add a “sale day” with percentages off and track who makes a profit.

    Learns: decimals, percentages, budgeting

    🎲1–2 hours

    Design-a-board-game

    Pick a topic they’re studying — the water cycle, Ancient Egypt — and design a board game about it. Playtest, fix the rules, play again.

    Learns: deep understanding, systems thinking, iteration

    🔐30 min

    Escape-room review

    Turn revision into locked boxes: each puzzle uses something from the last unit, and every answer unlocks the next clue.

    Learns: retrieval practice, problem-solving, collaboration

    🌉30 min

    Paper bridge challenge

    With only paper and tape, build a bridge between two books. Test it with coins: which shape holds the most? Redesign and beat your record.

    Learns: engineering, forces, persistence

    Turn any lesson into play — the 5-step recipe

    1. Start with a question

      Swap “Here’s the answer” for “What do you think will happen?”

    2. Hand it over

      Let kids touch, build, sort and test — minds-on and hands-on.

    3. Add a gentle goal

      A challenge or a secret rule to discover turns free play into guided play.

    4. Make mistakes cheap

      Retries are part of the game. Ask: “What will you try next?”

    5. Play together, then talk

      Explaining what they found is what locks the learning in.

    Good questions

    Grown-ups often ask…

    Curious, sceptical or both? Excellent — that’s the right attitude for learning anything.

    ?
    Isn’t play just… fun? Where’s the learning?

    Fun is the hook, not the goal. In guided play, an adult designs the activity around a learning goal and lets the child lead the exploring. Studies comparing it with direct instruction find equal or better results for many early skills — especially maths and shapes.

    Does this mean no more teaching or textbooks?

    No. Clear explanations, good books and practice still matter. The point is balance: many children spend far too much time listening and copying and too little time doing. Mix in active, playful formats and the teaching you do will stick better.

    My child has exams. Won’t play waste time?

    Quizzes, games and challenges are packed with retrieval practice — one of the most effective ways to prepare for tests. In one classic study, practising recall beat re-reading by 61% to 40% a week later. Playful revision is exam prep that kids don’t dread.

    Do educational apps and video games count?

    Some do. Check for the same five ingredients: does it make kids think (not just tap), connect to their world, let them try again, and involve other people? Learning games tend to work best alongside other teaching and when played with others.

    What age does this apply to?

    All ages. The richest research on play is in early childhood, but active, game-like learning also beats passive lecturing for older students — even at university.

    How much play is enough?

    There’s no magic number. A good rule of thumb: children should spend a big part of each day actively doing — building, testing, talking, moving — rather than passively receiving. And protect unstructured free play too: under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, play is a right, not a reward.

    Start small: one question instead of an answer, one game instead of a worksheet. Then watch what sticks.
